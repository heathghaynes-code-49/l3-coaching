Most senior leaders are over-invested in task management and severely under-invested in their people. They run tight agendas, protect the calendar, and optimize for output… yet they wonder why ownership is low, execution is inconsistent, and good people quietly disengage.

{% pullQuote "The formal structure is intact. It's the informal investment that's missing. And without both, you don't actually develop people — you manage them." %}

## The Two Types of Time Investment

The **Investing Time framework** draws a clear distinction between two categories of time that leaders spend with their teams:

**Formal / Structured Time** — your 1:1s, team meetings, performance reviews, training sessions, and structured updates. These interactions communicate that someone is a priority. They're where you transfer process, establish expectations, and create rhythm.

**Informal / Spontaneous Time** — an impromptu hallway conversation, coffee before a hard week, stopping by someone's office to check in (not to debrief a deliverable), a quick text of encouragement when someone's grinding through something difficult.

- You need both.
- It's about balance.
- Be intentional with how you allocate your time investing in your people.

Both types of investment serve a specific function. The problem is that most leaders have collapsed their development strategy into the first category alone and are wondering why it's not working.

## Why Formal Time Alone Isn't Enough

Formal time is efficient.

- It runs on agendas.
- It produces notes, action items, and accountability checkpoints.
- It's also the easiest to justify on a crowded calendar.

{% pullQuote "It tells your team what you expect. It doesn't build the trust required for them to tell you what's actually happening." %}

When every interaction is structured, people perform for the meeting. They bring their polished version. Questions they actually need answered stay unasked. Problems surface late, if at all. And the relationship stays transactional even when the words on the wall say otherwise.

Informal time breaks that pattern. It reduces self-preservation — that's the instinct people have to protect themselves in environments where vulnerability isn't safe. When you show up without an agenda, it signals something important: **you're here for the person, not just the output.**

That shift matters more than most leaders realize.

## What Each Investment Actually Produces

These two modes of time investment develop different things in your people.

Formal, structured time develops **hard skills, clarity, and accountability**. It creates the consistency that high-performing teams depend on. Regular 1:1s, structured coaching conversations, and defined development plans all belong here. This is how you communicate that growth is taken seriously.

Informal, spontaneous time develops **trust, psychological safety, and real ownership**.

When people experience you as someone who genuinely cares, not just someone who manages performance, they stop doing the minimum required to satisfy the process. They start taking initiative. They bring problems to you instead of hiding them. They recruit others into the work because they believe in it.

{% pullQuote "Leadership that liberates people requires both. The structure tells them where to go. The relationship gives them a reason to care." %}

## The Pattern Most Leaders Are Running

Here's what the default looks like in most organizations:

The formal structure exists. 1:1s are on the calendar. Team meetings happen. The communication cadence is technically sound. But when you look at how time actually gets spent inside those structures, it's almost entirely operational: updates, blockers, priorities, metrics.

Meanwhile, informal investment has been crowded out entirely. Lunches got replaced by desk-based working lunches. The casual check-in became a Slack message. The open door is technically open, but no one walks through it because the cultural signal is that everyone is too busy.

The result is a team that is functionally compliant but not genuinely engaged. They show up, they hit targets when conditions are right, and they wait to be told what to do next. **This is not a talent problem. It is a relational deficit.**

## What a Rebalanced Investment Actually Looks Like

Rebalancing doesn't require a calendar overhaul. It requires intention.

On the formal side, the question to ask is whether your structured time is actually developing people or just managing tasks. A 1:1 that only covers project status is not a development conversation. A team meeting that never addresses how people are functioning together is not a culture-building exercise. Formal time has to be intentionally invested, not just scheduled.

On the informal side, the bar is lower than most leaders assume. You don't need long conversations. You need consistent signals that the person matters outside of their deliverables. Stopping by someone's desk with no agenda. Noticing when someone seems off and following up. Sending a text when you know someone is dealing with something difficult. These investments **cost minutes and return trust**.

That's why L3 Leadership Coaching is direct on this point: leaders worth following are intentional to provide a balance of both, because each one builds something the other cannot.

## The Leadership Formation Question

Underneath the time investment question is a more fundamental one: what kind of leader are you forming yourself to be?

A leader who only shows up in structured contexts communicates, intentionally or not, that people are a means to an end. The relationship is functional. When results are good, everything is fine. When results suffer, the relationship suffers with it.

A leader who invests informally as well communicates something different: that the person has value apart from their performance. That dynamic builds the kind of loyalty and ownership that no management system can manufacture.

This is the progression that L3 focuses on: **you cannot liberate people you don't actually know.** And you cannot know them if every interaction is filtered through an agenda.

## A Practical Starting Point

Before your next week begins, answer these two questions honestly:

**Formal:** Are your structured touchpoints actually developing your people, or just managing workflow? If they're mostly operational, choose one recurring conversation and make it explicitly about the person's growth, challenges, and long-term trajectory.

**Informal:** Who on your team have you not had a genuinely informal interaction with recently? Not a Slack message. Not a quick pre-meeting check-in. A real conversation with no deliverable attached. Schedule a time with no set agenda. Show up, listen, and let the conversation flow naturally based on their needs.
